002   “Above Chance” Fails the Input-Blind Floor

009                                               Abstract
010
011             Multiple-choice scores are commonly interpreted against chance. We argue
012             that, before comparing model arms, each score should instead clear a re-
013
                porting floor given by the construct’s best constant input-blind predictor,
                and we scope the claim: this is a per-construct reporting convention, not
014
                a logical validity gate. Below that floor an interface’s raw arm differences
015
                need not be trusted in the same way as above it, but the mechanism by
016             which the floor matters differs by construct family — we therefore state the
017             floor’s role per family rather than assert a single necessary condition. The
018             floor is itself an estimate, and its calibrating null must be realisable by the
019             construct. Under a null restricted to each item’s legal letters, only MMLU
020             and BoolQ have label floors a balanced null could not plausibly produce;
021             the other letter constructs show that chance is the wrong reference without
022             establishing that their floors differ from chance in magnitude.
023             On MMLU-Pro, the reference choice changes the reading of structurally
024             damaged arms. Under the matched paired-bootstrap standard, only 1/12
025             non-OLMo cells clears the best-constant floor, and the point-estimate sen-
026
                sitivity to winner treatment is reported separately rather than mixed into
                that verdict. Across five smaller benchmarks, only 9/85 designated dam-
027
                aged cells clear the floor on the interval standard, all of them healed OLMo-
028
                2 arms. The complete matched-standard counts are consolidated in Table 1;
029             a power analysis explains why most per-benchmark tests remain inconclu-
030             sive.
031             For the distinct question of whether one arm carries item-level information,
032             we adapt an arm-conditional permutation null within option-count strata.
033             It assigns every pure constant emitter exactly zero regardless of collapse
034             letter. Re-judging 27 cells withdraws one above-floor capability label, dis-
035             solves both below-floor competence labels, and exposes a weak intact-family
036             anchor. Full-fp32 evaluation removes every bf16 exact tie yet does not re-
037             cover accuracy, ruling out numerical precision as the explanation. Together
038             these results motivate a simple rule: calibrate every reported construct to
039
                its explicit input-blind null before interpreting arm differences.

      1     Introduction
042
043   A recent multilingual evaluation reported an awkward pattern: every tested model was
044   above chance, yet none beat the benchmark’s majority-class baseline (Arčon et al., 2026).
045   The authors found the flip because they happened to print both reference lines. This is no
046   curiosity of one newly constructed benchmark: a systematic construct-validity review of 445
047   language-model benchmarks offers 27 actionable checklist items, none of which asks authors
048   to report a null, a chance level, or a constant predictor (Bean et al., 2025). The missing
049
      item is operational: before comparing model arms, test whether the reported construct
      clears its own input-blind floor. Figure 1 previews the two distinct nulls needed for interface
050
      comparability and arm-specific item-level information.
051
      For a letter-valued multiple-choice construct, the floor is the most accurate constant letter
052
      under the benchmark’s empirical gold marginal; for content likelihood, an analogous input-
053
      blind heuristic can exploit option length. These references need not equal nominal chance,

084   We study null calibration as a measurement protocol, not a new multiple-choice interface
085
      or debiasing algorithm. Majority-class baselines for MCQA already exist (Balepur et al.,
      2024); letter-versus-content formulations are established (Gu et al., 2025; Cho et al., 2026);
086
      constant outputs are known to game automatic judges (Zheng et al., 2025). Our contribution
087
      is to make the construct-appropriate input-blind null a pre-comparison gate, quantify the
088   degrees of freedom hidden inside it, and separate two questions needing different references:
089
      1. Is the interface valid for arm comparison? Use an arm-independent best-constant floor.
090   2. Does this particular arm carry item-level information? Use an arm-conditional permu-
091       tation null that preserves the arm’s prediction marginal.
092   The distinction prevents two opposite mistakes: chance can credit a literal constant emitter
093   as competent, while the best-constant floor can label two equally empty emitters differently
094   solely for collapsing onto different letters. The first motivates our headline floor; the second
095   motivates read-out v2, for which we prove constant-collapse invariance algebraically.
096   Our evidence uses ten target constructs plus Winogrande as a negative control, four base-
097   model families, letter and content likelihood interfaces, and structurally damaged arms. Our
098   findings:

102       and BoolQ support a quantitative above-balanced-null claim.

          tokenizer are fixed. Table 8 reports the measured reversals and spans; Section A.1.5
          records the separate option-count correction.

121   2   Related Work
122   Input-blind and constant baselines. Balepur et al. (2024) define a majority-class baseline
123   for letter MCQA and recommend stronger-than-chance references; their object is dataset
124   cheatability under a black-box generative setup, ours a per-arm gate for likelihood-scored
125   constructs. The distinction is operational: their invalid-output analysis imputes either 0.0
126   or 0.25 on MMLU, while the construct’s best-constant letter value is 0.2689. Zheng et al.
127   (2025) use “null model” for crafted constant responses that adversarially exploit an LLM
128   judge — an attack optimized against a judge, whereas ours is a reference derived from
129   benchmark statistics, requiring no attacker. The metalinguistic preprint motivating our
130
      opening reports the above-chance/below-majority pattern descriptively on one new bench-
      mark, without turning it into a pre-comparison protocol (Arčon et al., 2026).
131
132   Selection bias and MC interfaces. Letter preference is not a new observation. Zheng et al.
133   (2024) identify selection and token bias and propose PriDe, which estimates an option-ID
134   prior from permuted contents. PriDe fixes the predictor; null calibration fixes the reference
135   line. Even after prediction debiasing, reporting only against chance can leave the bench-
136   mark’s gold-marginal floor unreported. We therefore do not reject permutation controls on
137   cost grounds — they are a useful and potentially inexpensive diagnostic, answering whether
138   predictions should be corrected, while our arm-independent floor answers whether the re-
139
      sulting interface score is comparable across arms.
140   OLMES standardizes both multiple-choice formulation (MCF) and cloze formulation (CF),
141
      then uses the better score per task and model (Gu et al., 2025); this is not a size-keyed
      rule. Its reference discussion is nevertheless framed around a random baseline rather than
142
      a measured label-marginal floor, so the max rule can select an interface without testing
143
      whether that interface clears an input-blind reference. Our ARC-Easy result shows CF
144   rescuing an at-floor letter read-out; MMLU shows that an interface swap need not do so.
145   Cho et al. (2026) independently study symbols, cloze, and hybrid formats and propose a
146   new question-sensitive score. We do not claim the interface contrast; our method instead
147   gates whichever construct is reported.
148
149
      Length-based scoring. Length-normalized likelihood can over-correct and its token-based
      form is tokenizer-dependent (Oostermeijer, 2026), so we claim no generic length sensitivity
150
      or tokenizer dependence of the scoring rule. We show the induced input-blind floor inherits
151
      these choices: its value changes with tie convention, character versus continuation-token
152   length, and tokenizer. OLMES’s observation that tokenizer dependence may be irrelevant
153   when ranking options for one fixed model and tokenizer is compatible with ours; our scope
154   is cross-model comparison of the reference itself.
155
156   Nulls as interpretive controls. Our arm-conditional read-out v2 is a chance-corrected agree-
157   ment statistic: ∆perm equals the numerator of Cohen’s κ (Cohen, 1960) within option-count
158
      strata, and the constant-emitter zero we rely on is that literature’s standard property, not
      a new result. We claim only the stratification by variable option count and the statistic’s
159
      use as a pre-comparison gate.
160

283   3.3   Four under-specifications of the reference
284   A content floor is not a dataset constant. Four independent choices must be printed with
285   the score: the tie convention, the length unit, the tokenizer, and — when option count
286   varies — the definition of chance. In our audit, tie handling can reverse an ARC-Challenge
287   verdict, and the extreme-convention sensitivity span on MMLU-Pro is much larger than
288   the operative span among defensible conventions; changing the length unit or tokenizer also
289
      moves the floor by more than the effects under study. Nominal and item-averaged chance
      likewise disagree on MMLU-Pro. Tables 7 and 8 place the exact floors, tie rates, gaps,
290
      sensitivity endpoints, and calibrated verdicts in aligned columns; Appendix A.1 develops
291
      each choice and its provenance. Jointly, the four define a token content floor as a property
292   of (dataset, convention, unit, tokenizer). The letter floor is exempt: it depends only on the
293   item set’s gold-label marginal.

457   A reporting rule. Define and report the strongest construct-appropriate input-blind pre-
458   dictor, test each arm against it, and place the floor label beside every comparison. The

898   A.2   Prior art on option-count-aware chance correction
899   Section 3.2 claims the within-stratum permutation and its use as a gate, and nothing else.
900   This appendix attributes the rest, result by result. Bennett’s S sets pe = 1/k for k response
901   categories (Bennett et al., 1954); Brennan & Prediger (1981) recommend exactly that free-
902   marginal alternative wherever κ’s marginal dependence misleads; formula scoring corrects
903   for guessing as an explicit function of the per-item option count (Frary, 1988); and Brenner
904   & Kliebsch (1996) show that a weighted κ drifts systematically with the number of cate-
905   gories — the very pathology our stratification is designed to avoid. Pooling an agreement
906   coeﬀicient across heterogeneous item groups is likewise established (Vries et al., 2008). We
907
      therefore claim none of the following: correcting for the number of options, choosing pe as a
      methodological decision, or noticing that κ-family statistics depend on k. The works above
908
      assume a single global k; what we are not aware of in that literature is a null in which
909
      k varies item to item (n_opt ∈ {3, . . . , 10}, eight strata here), with the permutation con-
910   fined within strata so that a letter illegal for an item can never be credited to it. That is a
911   loophole-closing construction, not a new statistic, and its measured footprint on MMLU-Pro
912   is the 36 items quantified in Appendix A.3.
913

       A.14   Reporting checklist
1446   For each reported multiple-choice construct, we recommend the following minimal record:
1447
       1. Define the input-blind predictor family and report the best member on the evaluated
1448      item set.
1449
       2. Report the score, null value, paired effect, confidence interval, and test.
1450
1451   3. For content length nulls, print tie convention, length unit, and tokenizer. If option count
1452      varies, print the definition of chance.
1453   4. Establish that the experiment could resolve the effect under discussion; otherwise describe
1454      the result as a non-observation.
1455   5. Keep arm-independent interface validity separate from arm-conditional item-level infor-
1456      mation.
1457   6. Treat a floor pass as necessary, not suﬀicient.
